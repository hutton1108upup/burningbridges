How to Make a Ghostface AI Video

To make a Ghostface AI video, choose a compatible horror template or build a bedroom image and animate it with an image-to-video tool. Prepare a clear portrait, keep the scene simple, then check the face and reveal before exporting. This guide covers both workflows and includes an original prompt you can adapt.

1. Choose how you want to make the video

The recognizable idea is a relaxed phone call interrupted by a masked presence. Our two Ghostface video references show the scene; watch the ending as well as the opening before picking a tool.

A dedicated template

Choose this route when you want the scene shown in a provider’s example with fewer creative decisions. Open the effect page, compare its preview, read its photo requirements and check the displayed cost before submitting. Make sure you are selecting a video effect, not a still-image filter.

An image-to-video workflow

Choose this route when you want to design the room, wardrobe or camera angle yourself. You first need a usable scene image, then a tool that can animate it. A portrait alone and a fully composed bedroom image are different starting points. Confirm whether your chosen tool takes a face reference, a first frame, or both.

2. Prepare one clear portrait

Pick a sharp photo with the eyes visible and enough space around the head to inspect the face. Avoid starting with a tiny crop or an image that already has distorted features. Use a photo you own or have permission to use.

Open our local portrait preview to compare candidate photos. It accepts JPG, PNG and WebP up to 10 MB; those are this site’s limits, not a promise about an external service. Choosing a photo here does not upload it or send it to another tool.

Save the selected original somewhere you can find it. In the external generator, check the accepted format and input instructions again. See our photo preparation guide if your image is blurry, heavily filtered or cropped too tightly.

3. Prepare the bedroom scene before animating

For the custom route, compose an opening image with the caller in the foreground and space behind them for the second figure. Keep the phone receiver clear of the eyes. A doorway visible over one shoulder gives you a place for the reveal without moving the camera.

Inspect that still closely. Are there two recognizable subjects? Does the receiver connect naturally to the hand? Does the doorway belong to the same room? Fixing the starting image gives you a cleaner basis for judging the animation.

Runway’s official image-to-video guide explains that the input image establishes the composition and appearance, while the text prompt describes movement. It also cautions that visual defects in a source image can become more noticeable in video. The workflow here uses that distinction; it is not a claim that Runway offers a Ghostface template.

4. Use a short Ghostface AI video prompt

Once the opening image looks right, describe the change you want to see. This original example assumes the image already contains the caller, phone and masked figure. It is a starting point for experimentation, not a tested preset or a guarantee of a matching result.

The camera stays in a fixed medium shot. The caller smiles while holding the phone receiver, then pauses and glances toward the doorway. The masked figure takes one slow step forward. The caller notices and turns toward the camera with a startled expression. Keep the movement restrained and the lighting steady throughout the shot.

Copy the paragraph into the motion prompt field of a compatible tool. Set a portrait frame if that is the format you need, review the generation settings and cost, then submit one draft. Field names, duration choices and prices differ by service.

Leave out bubble gum, flickering lights and a rapid camera move in the first attempt. Once the basic interaction works, add one extra action and compare the next result. This makes it easier to identify which instruction changed the clip. If your tool declines the request, follow its stated content rules or choose another supported scene.

5. Review the draft before generating more

Watch once at normal speed, then pause at the moment the caller turns. Compare the opening and final frames. Use the checklist below to decide whether to adjust the source image, prompt or edit.

The face changes during the turn
Try a smaller head movement and inspect whether the starting image clearly shows the face. Compare another draft before adding more actions.
The hand merges with the phone
Check the still image first. Give the receiver and fingers more separation, and keep the hand still in the next motion prompt.
The figure appears too early
Reduce the movement in the opening shot. If exact timing matters, consider making a separate reveal shot and placing the cut where you want it in an editor.
The clip cuts away from the room
Remove instructions that suggest a new camera angle. Test one continuous view with fewer events before attempting a longer sequence.
The result looks like a moving photo
Specify one visible action, such as a glance or a step, and check whether the chosen workflow animates the scene or only applies a camera effect.

These are troubleshooting suggestions, not measured success rates. Save the prompt and settings beside each draft so you can compare changes without relying on memory.

6. Finish the sound and export

Decide where the quiet setup ends and the scare lands. If the tool produces audio, listen for unwanted speech or abrupt changes. If it exports a silent clip, add sound separately in an editor using audio you are permitted to use.

Preview the final file on a phone before sharing. Check that the face stays inside the crop, captions remain readable, and the closing frame is not accidentally trimmed. Review the export screen for resolution and watermark options before paying for another render; this guide does not promise a free export or a particular output quality.

Keep the editable project and an original export. If you later change the audio or opening caption, you will not need to rebuild the whole video.

Ghostface AI video FAQ

Can I make a Ghostface AI video from one photo?

A dedicated template may accept one portrait. A custom image-to-video workflow can require a composed starting image instead. Check the tool’s actual input requirements before purchasing.

What is the difference between a Ghostface AI photo and video?

A photo is a single image. A video adds movement over time. Check that the selected effect produces a video file rather than only a still or a slideshow.

Is there a free Ghostface AI video maker here?

Watching the references and using the local portrait preview are free. This site does not offer Ghostface generation. Check external tools for their current render and export costs.

Can I use this prompt in any AI video generator?

The prompt is an original creative starting point, not a universal preset. It assumes a compatible image-to-video tool and an opening image containing the subjects. Features and content rules differ between tools.

How do I keep the same face throughout the clip?
